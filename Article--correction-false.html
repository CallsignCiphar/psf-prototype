Air defence in depth: how Pakistan is layering its western approaches

Radar coverage, interceptor basing and ground-based systems are being stitched into one picture. The gaps that remain are mostly about time.

ARKM
Ayesha RahmanandAir Cdre (R) Kamran MalikPSF Expert
16 Oct 2026, 09:00 PKTUpdated 17 Oct 20269 min read
Quetta, Pakistan
Key takeaways
  • Long-range radar coverage of the western approaches is now continuous, but low-altitude gaps remain in mountainous terrain.
  • Interceptor basing has shifted west, cutting reaction times for the Quetta and Peshawar sectors.
  • The weakest link is data fusion: sensors exist, but sharing one picture across services is still partly manual.

For most of the last two decades, Pakistan's air defence planning faced east. The threat that shaped radar siting, airbase hardening and interceptor deployment came from one direction, and the western border was treated as a counter-terrorism problem rather than an air defence one.

That has changed. Drone incursions, the movement of armed groups across the Durand Line and a more crowded regional airspace have pushed planners to treat the west as a front in its own right.

Coverage is continuous. Response is not.

According to two officials familiar with the programme, long-range surveillance radar now provides continuous coverage of the western approaches at medium and high altitude.1 Low-altitude coverage is the harder problem: mountain ridges create shadows that a small drone can use for most of its route.

Gap-filler radars and passive sensors are being deployed to close those shadows, but each new sensor adds data that has to be fused, filtered and passed to someone who can act on it.

"The sensors are there. What we are still building is the habit of trusting another service's picture."

Senior officer, speaking on condition of anonymity

Interceptor basing has also moved. Rotating detachments to bases closer to the western border cuts the time between detection and engagement, which matters most against slow, low-flying targets that give little warning.

What to watch

The next test is integration. If the services can share one air picture in real time, the western approaches will be covered in depth for the first time.
